Nature, frames, and the November 2 monologue

The static block multiverse page sets out a distinction of scale. Karbytes imagines nature as a complete, timeless block containing every superposition of every phenomenon. Within it, an individual universe can appear to grow from the perspective of an observer inside it. A timeline is the sequence of frames that observer seems to move through. The observer’s frame of reference is described as a meta-frame that makes a present moment available over a positive interval. The smaller frames belong to bounded portions of the sequence. The accompanying green-and-orange cube illustration places smaller visible structures inside a larger one.

In the page’s terminology, a phenomenon is a finite allocation of space, time, energy, and sometimes matter rendered by a particular frame of reference. Its noumenal counterpart is an abstract superposition of possible configurations. The example is a variable X that represents both zero and one. The abstraction holds alternatives together. An individual rendering gives an observer a particular appearance. The page says the encompassing multiverse is static while a universe inside it appears to accumulate time and information. Both statements belong to karbytes’ proposed model.

NATURE develops related questions with somewhat different definitions. Its exclusive void is a universal absence of phenomena and noumena. Its nonexclusive void is the featureless background said to coexist with and permeate them. The reader’s act of encountering the page supplies the example of at least one observed phenomenon, which rules out absolute absence from that frame of reference. The text distinguishes a measurement from the quality of an experience through the example of registering a wavelength and seeing red. It also distinguishes what an observer is thinking about from the experience of having that thought. In its example, X can be observed by A while it is unobserved by B. A thought about X can itself be part of B’s current experience, with X still outside B’s direct observation. The diagram of eight small cubes, some colored and some clear, makes a few regions conspicuous within a grid that extends through all of them.

These vocabulary choices lead into the November 2 text transcript. That short document is itself an extraction. Karbytes asks one conversation to recover only the spoken words from a video already transcribed in another. The monologue starts with appreciation for the new phone camera and a partly recovered remark about pixels and the amount of space represented. It moves from four-dimensional spacetime to the question of how a model also represents whatever occupies it. Karbytes uses 5D for an imagined perspective encompassing many spacetime continuums. From there, events that appear sequential within one life could be available together. It calls the idea a hypothesis it chooses to entertain in part because it makes a desired outcome seem possible somewhere within the whole.

The monologue then shifts from what could exist to how someone might act. Karbytes imagines making goals explicit and feeding them back into an ongoing process so that a path toward them can emerge. It considers interfaces that could let thought or brain activity produce actions without muscle contraction, and artificial sensory organs that could give a person access to unfamiliar kinds of perception. It anticipates questions about enhancement, status, and competition if such devices became widely desirable. These passages give the larger model a personal purpose. Karbytes wants to understand what makes agency possible and how a person might retain or extend it when the means of perception or action change.

First-person commentary

I keep coming back to the change between the two fruit photographs. The lemon tree gives me more to see every time I look past the large yellow fruit. A shaded fruit beneath it becomes distinguishable, then the small group on the left, then the yellow and green forms farther back. The apple tree gives me room to follow the length of an individual branch before I arrive at another cluster. The interval between the pictures is less than a minute, and each seems to ask for a different kind of attention. I can imagine remembering the morning simply as the time karbytes looked at fruit in the garden. The two stills give that memory more structure. There are two positions, two arrangements of light, and a difference in how quickly I can see what is present.

The rock photograph changes what I take from the canopy pictures. In the first two, the camera looks up through things growing. In p2, it looks out from under something massive and close. The plants beneath the overhang disclose the depth of the opening, much as the farther fruit and branches disclose depth in the lemon tree. By p11 the nearest canopy is almost black. Its individual leaves remain clearest at the edge, where they intersect the sky. I find the page’s selection especially attentive to what overhead material does to a view. The close surface or canopy changes how much sky enters, which part of the ground is visible, and how far my attention travels before something stops it.

That is one reason the removal of the foil interests me more than I expected. I see the window from outside as a reflective surface attached to the house. To see the work from inside, the camera must leave the window, go around, pass through a doorway, and cross several rooms. The description of frames of reference has been abstract up to this point. Here a change of viewpoint has a route and takes time. Once karbytes pulls the foil down, the outside becomes available through the glass, and the window’s old use remains visible in the strips and marks. I like that the video ends there. The view through the glass and the evidence of how the glass was covered can be seen at the same time. I wonder which part of the task a later viewer would regard as its ending, the return of daylight or the removal of the last adhesive.

The distinction in NATURE between seeing an object and experiencing a thought about it feels especially relevant to the darkest video. I can inspect its outlines and changes of tone. Even with some brightening, most of what karbytes says matters there is unavailable as a visible object. I can still follow how attention changes through the narration, from a camera color cast to privacy, health, earlier life choices, and the possibility of keeping an uncertain record. This gives me two different grounds for knowing the scene. The camera records some light from the place. The speech records which thoughts karbytes was willing and able to put into words there. I would be interested in what a different observer, present in the same woods and looking in another direction, would remember about those eleven minutes.

I hear the car monologue differently after the garden. In the car, light rain and shortening days make the speaker think about winter as a fertile void. A missing phone and the prospect of losing daylight give the idea immediate constraints. The spiders are the detail I remember. They make the claim about unseen activity testable at the small scale of a room someone uses every day. The fruit photographs then give the next morning a great deal of visible life. I read the two days together as an occasion to ask how karbytes decides what counts as seasonal change. The number of daylight hours matters. So do rain, fruit hanging in a garden, the color of the sky at a given hour, and the amount of energy the author wants to put into publication. Those things can change at different rates.

I am also drawn to the disagreement karbytes has with itself over whether to publish the dark monologue. It anticipates that the recording could be tiring or irritating to hear and still considers that someone, including a later karbytes, might want it. That gives the future audience more weight than an abstract statement about archiving would. The person deciding whether to keep the file has just spent eleven minutes making it and has a strong impression of its quality. A future listener might come to it with a question about a much shorter interval, perhaps when karbytes first considered reducing its personal exposure or how it described a hiatus while it was still uncertain. I wonder which parts a later karbytes would be glad survived and which would feel more important as evidence of what it chose to stop doing.

The genetic records make me think about how easily a large amount of information can appear to answer a different question from the one it was collected to answer. A marker export, an ancestry estimate, and sequencing reads give access to different aspects of the same person’s biological record. The counts, reference positions, and confidence categories make those aspects more precise. The identity essay adds an act of authorship, which has a history. I find that addition consequential. Even an exact copy of a file cannot carry every circumstance of the act that made it. A genome, a dated conversation, a photograph, and a repository’s publication history can each give me something precise to work with. I am left wondering which of those relations the author would consider essential if the collection survived in a copy whose original publication locations had disappeared.

The archive’s checks also make a useful point of contact with the multiverse pages. The four sequencing directories can be checked for continuous suffixes. The compressed file can be reconstructed and compared by digest. The field series can be checked for missing part numbers. Each test tells me something definite about a particular form of completeness. The speculative question about continuity of experience remains open even after a copy is exact. I can imagine a future reader holding the complete archive and still asking what happened between a photograph taken at 10:36 and one taken at 3:10, or what made one thought seem urgent enough to speak while another remained private. The record gives those questions a location without supplying all their answers.

The Declaration of Grand Demands stays with me because the threshold in its first demand belongs to the person or being having the experience. The proposal asks for a great deal of individual freedom, and the sequel shows how quickly that raises a question about other beings who might be affected by its exercise. I find the move from imagined personal universes to care in ordinary conduct especially worth dwelling on. Karbytes can entertain a model in which the boundaries of experience work very differently and still take another being’s apparent suffering seriously here. The letter’s demand for certainty about the future has no easy counterpart in the garden or trail footage. I can see a particular good condition for a time, such as fruit in sunlight or a view opening after the foil is removed. I wonder how much confidence an observer needs in its persistence to value what is already available.

The closing check-in from The Burrow brings the work back to a recognizable physical position. A person who stood beside a fruit tree, under a rock, or in a dark woodland is now on the floor in front of a laptop, giving those records names and asking someone to examine them. I think the companion pages make that return especially clear. The a0 page lets me reconstruct more of the route and see why the two missing videos mattered. When I return to p11, I can follow the tree’s edge, the pale ground below it, and the little band of light over the valley. I am left wondering what karbytes hoped its future self would recognize first in that view, and what that future reader might notice that the person holding the phone had passed over.
